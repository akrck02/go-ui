📱 Go UI on Android

Status: a first step (October 2026). A Go UI program opens and draws on an Android 16 emulator, with a 3D scene under its interface, and reacts to taps and drags; the same code runs on the desktop and in a browser. The example looks like Android’s own apps (Material Design 3). This page started as a plan (what to build, install and try, in which order); What was built says how it turned out, and Next what is left.

There is no Java and no golang.org/x/mobile. The first step used x/mobile (gomobile); its way of calling OpenGL (every call to another thread, three at a time) was about two thirds of what a 3D view cost on a phone, and Go Renderer’s own activity halved it (see A cap on frames).

Touch. The first finger down is the pointer: it moves the cursor and presses the left button, lifting it releases the button, so every control works unchanged. Other fingers are ignored until it lifts. Positions are divided by the screen’s density, so layout pixels are Android’s density-independent pixels (1⁄160 inch) and an interface is the same size on every phone.

Drawing on demand. As on the desktop and in the browser, the window draws only when something happens: a touch, a size change, a frame a control asks for (RequestFrame) or window.Wake. A frame that would look like the one shown is not drawn. Idle on a phone, the program uses nothing at all: the drawing thread sleeps until a touch or a frame asked for.

A cap on frames. Phones refresh at 90 or 120 Hz, and a finger dragging a 3D view asks for a frame at every touch event (over a hundred a second). The window draws at most Options.MaxFramesPerSecond frames a second, 60 on a phone by default; the system’s own repaints do not count. Measured on a Pixel 6 Pro dragging the example’s island for 10 seconds: well over a core at first (an independent measurement found 125–128 % at about 100 frames a second); 82 % with the cap and lighter frames, still through x/mobile; 41 % with Go Renderer’s own activity and direct OpenGL calls.

The system’s bars. The window covers the whole screen, under see-through bars, as Android’s own apps do. Context.Insets() says how much of each edge the status bar and the gesture bar cover, in layout pixels, so a program paints its own bars’ backgrounds under them and keeps its controls clear. The window asks Android through JNI (getRootWindowInsets); before the window is shown it falls back on the bars’ standard heights. The window theme in cmd/mobile/android/res makes the bars see-through, with dark icons in the system’s light theme and light ones in its dark theme.

When it closes on a phone nobody can reach. On Android the window keeps the report of a crash (an unrecovered panic, a fatal error or signal, with the failing goroutine’s stack) in the application’s own folder, through runtime/debug.SetCrashOutput, and window.LastCrash() gives it to the next run; window.Graphics() names the phone’s OpenGL ES and GPU, or the error that stopped the window drawing. cmd/mobile shows both in its settings, and warns on its first screen after a crash, so a screenshot is enough to know what happened.

The dark theme. window.SystemPrefersDark() tells whether the phone (or the browser, or the computer) is set to a dark theme, so a program can follow it; cmd/mobile asks at every frame until a theme is chosen in its settings.

Typing and notifications. While a text field is typed in (Context.Typing), the window brings the phone’s on-screen keyboard up, and puts it away after. The keyboard edits the field’s text, as on the phone’s own fields: it suggests words from it, underlines the word it is composing, and its suggestion replaces that word (Context.TypedField, Context.EditTypedField). window.Notify shows a notification in the phone’s shade (in a browser, through the page’s notifications), once window.NotificationsAllowed; window.AskForNotifications asks the person. On the desktop they are not shown yet. window.OpenLink(link) opens a link in the application that shows it, window.Share(title, text) offers a text through the system’s sheet, Context.Copy and Context.ClipboardText reach the phone’s clipboard, and window.Preference / window.KeepPreference keep small values between runs (a JSON file in the application’s folder; on the desktop, the user’s configuration folder; in a browser, local storage). Options.Hidden is called when the program leaves the screen, the moment to keep them, since Android may end the program after that; Options.Shown is called when it comes back (in a browser, when the page shows again), the moment to play again what Hidden stopped, such as a sound. The person’s text size reaches every text through Context.SetTextScale, which the window sets in every frame. window.OpenVideo(film) plays a film’s pictures (not yet its sound) in a draw2d.Image that Context.Image draws: Play, Pause, Loop, and Update in every frame it shows. On Android the phone’s decoders read it; in a browser, a video element the page never shows; on the desktop it is ErrVideoUnsupported for now. window.FeelLongPress gives the short buzz of a long press (Context.LongPressed), where the person has touch feedback on; in a browser it vibrates where the browser can. window.Feel gives the other buzzes a phone’s applications give: FeelingTick when something is chosen, FeelingConfirm when something went through, FeelingReject when something went wrong or needs a look.

The wallpaper’s colours. Android 12 and later make five palettes from the wallpaper (Material You), and the system’s own applications take their colours from them. window.SystemPalettes() returns them, thirteen shades each, from white to black (window.PaletteTones), and false on an older phone, on the desktop and in a browser. They change with the wallpaper, so read them at every frame. Go UI for Android’s material.System builds a Material scheme from them.

A scene under the interface. Options.Scene returns, at every step, a scene and a camera to draw behind the interface, or nil for none. It gets the input the interface did not claim, so a drag on an empty part of the screen turns the camera while a drag on a slider moves the slider. The scene is drawn by Go Renderer’s gles.SceneRenderer on Android and in browsers, and its OpenGL scene renderer on the desktop.

cmd/mobile is built the way Android’s own apps look, out of Go UI boxes, texts and clickables (material.go): a top app bar and a navigation bar painted under the system’s bars, list items with a round icon and a Material switch, filter chips, pill-shaped buttons, cards and floating buttons, in Material Design 3’s baseline colours, light and dark, and in Roboto, Android’s typeface (embedded under the SIL Open Font License, in cmd/mobile/fonts, and given to the window as Options.Fonts). Its three destinations are a list of lighthouses with their details, a map with the 3D island (drag to turn, buttons to come closer, a slider for the time of day) and settings (the dark theme, the screen’s size and insets).

Screen readers

TalkBack reads a Go UI program as it reads Android’s own applications: a finger moved over the screen reads what is under it, a swipe goes to the next thing, a double tap presses it, and the thing read is ringed. Every frame, while a screen reader is on, the interface makes its list of nodes (screen readers); window/android.go numbers them as Android’s virtual views, says what Android widget each reads as (a switch is an android.widget.Switch) and hands them to Go Renderer, which sends them to one small Java class only when they change. What TalkBack asks (press, long press, scroll, the thing it is on) comes back the same way.

A package needs that class: go-ui-apk in Go UI for Android compiles it in. A package made without it (cmd/mobile/build-apk.sh, which carries no Java) runs as before, without a screen reader.

Building and trying it

export ANDROID_HOME=…/android-sdk ANDROID_NDK_HOME=$ANDROID_HOME/ndk/<version>
cmd/mobile/build-apk.sh mobile.apk
adb install -r mobile.apk
adb shell am start -n io.github.akrck02.goui.mobile/android.app.NativeActivity
adb exec-out screencap -p > screen.png          # what the screen shows
adb shell input tap 230 330                     # a tap, in screen pixels

build-apk.sh builds the program as a shared library with the NDK’s compiler (aligned to 16 KB pages, which newer phones need), and packs it with the Android SDK’s tools (aapt2, zipalign, apksigner) under cmd/mobile/android/AndroidManifest.xml: Android’s own NativeActivity, no Java code. It signs with the debug key: for trying, not for publishing.

Installed for this: the NDK (r29) through sdkmanager. The SDK, its build tools, the emulator and an arm64 Android 16 image were already there.

What was checked, and what was not

On the emulator (Android 16, arm64, 1080 × 2400 at 420 dots per inch): the window draws with sharp text at a density of 2.625, clear of the status bar and the gesture bar (49 and 24 layout pixels); a tap on a row opens its details and a tap on its switch flips it without opening it; the back arrow, the chips, the buttons, the navigation bar and the sliders work; the island draws at any time of day, turns with a drag and comes closer with the buttons; the dark theme switches every screen. Idle, the program uses about 0.5 % of a core; the map takes 16 draw calls a frame.

On a phone (Pixel 6 Pro, Android 16, Mali-G78, 1080 × 2340): the same, in the system’s dark theme at start, and after going to the background and back. That phone gives an OpenGL ES 3.2 context (first asked for version 2, through x/mobile). It found two faults the emulator hid, now fixed: the 3D map lost its ground after coming back from the background (meshes kept the buffers of the destroyed graphics context), and the program read the system’s theme before Android gave it.

Not yet: rotating the screen, and how it feels on other phones and tablets.

Risks

  • Phones differ. The window was tried on a Mali GPU (Pixel 6 Pro) and the emulator; an Adreno phone closed the first version, for a reason not known yet (the crash report will tell).
  • Battery: drawing on demand is a must on a phone, and it is in place, with a cap of 60 frames a second.
